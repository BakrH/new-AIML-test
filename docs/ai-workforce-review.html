Implementation review · Manual v1.0

BCS AI Workforce Review

An assessment of the draft operating manual against the actual plan — ProposalApp marketing through fulfillment first, then BCS-CG GovCon, then CMMC CISO delivery — with a task-by-task judgment on what a two-to-three-person team should hand to agents and what it must keep.

Reviewed 8 Sep 2026 Source BCS_AI_Workforce_Implementation_Manual_1.docx Staffing assumed 2–3 humans + agents per project
Verdict

The manual is architecturally sound and operationally too heavy for the team that has to run it. Its core design decisions are the right ones and unusually well chosen: a hard CUI/FCI boundary declared before anything else, a shared work record with structured handoffs instead of passing chat history between models, and an approval matrix that separates drafting from sending from committing. Those three choices are what most AI-workforce plans get wrong, and they are the reason this plan is worth building on rather than rewriting.

What it does not survive is its own headcount. The manual names nine distinct human roles and twenty-six sequential build gates, then hands them to two or three people who also have to sell, deliver, and keep the lights on. Roughly forty to sixty hours of governance labor lands before a single draft is produced, separation of duties collapses the moment one person wears three hats, and the thirteen-system tool stack carries recurring cost against pilot volumes too small to justify it.

Two substantive content gaps matter more than any of that. The demo and fulfillment stages of ProposalApp — the user's own stated phase-one scope — are absent from the manual entirely, and fulfillment is where the delegation return actually lives. Fixing that surfaces a contradiction the draft never resolves: agents are forbidden from touching sensitive data, yet white-glove proposal production is the deliverable. That needs a tiered data model, not a blanket prohibition.

01

What holds up under pressure

Five decisions in the draft should not be touched during revision. They are load-bearing.

  • The prohibition list is specific enough to be enforceable. Naming CUI, FCI, export-controlled data, credentials, network diagrams, vulnerability findings, SSPs and POA&Ms individually — rather than saying "sensitive information" — is what makes the rule testable. An agent can be evaluated against it.
  • Business state lives in systems of record, not in model memory. "Models do not exchange private chat histories" is the single most important sentence in the manual. It is what lets you swap a model, resume after a failure, and audit a decision six months later.
  • Autonomy is separated into four distinct authorities — draft, update internal records, send externally, approve commitments. Most plans conflate the last two and discover the problem when an agent quotes a price.
  • Every A–Z step carries completion evidence. Not "configure Composio" but "connection inventory and successful access tests." That converts a plan into something a sponsor can actually sign off.
  • "Human review time" is a tracked measure with the rule "automation must reduce total effort." This is the metric that catches the failure mode where agents produce more work than they remove. Keep it, and make it a gate rather than a report.
02

What breaks at three people

Eight findings, each with the smallest change that resolves it. Identifiers are for reference, not sequence — F-4 is the most consequential.

F-1

Nine roles, three people, no separation of duties

The manual assigns work to an executive sponsor, AI operations administrator, revenue operations owner, security reviewer, ProposalApp sales owner, knowledge owner, technical owner, operations owner, and system administrator. At two or three co-workers, one person is simultaneously the agent owner requesting a change, the technical owner making it, and the security reviewer approving it. The approval ceremony still runs; it just stops meaning anything.

FixCollapse to three hats — SPONSOR (offer, pricing, autonomy, production authorization), OPERATOR (agent config, knowledge, runtime, evals), REVENUE (pipeline, sends, relationship ownership) — and publish a short list of actions that may never be self-approved regardless of hats: expanding an agent's write scope, expanding autonomy, sending a first-touch message, and resuming after a sensitive-information event. Those four need a second named human, and if only two people exist, the sponsor is always the second.
F-2

Twenty-six gates before the first unit of value

The A–Z sequence and the ten-day plan put the first approved outreach at day ten, and the first agent-produced deliverable of any kind at day seven. Everything before that is charter writing, namespace creation, OAuth configuration, and runtime deployment. For a small team, that is a large unpaid bet placed before anyone has seen whether an agent-written account brief is actually good enough to use.

FixInsert a seventy-two-hour thin slice ahead of step A: one specialist, one task type (account research producing a sourced brief), the work record as a spreadsheet, approvals as a message in an existing channel, no runtime, no Composio, no charter. Run five real target accounts through it. Then build the control plane around what actually worked — and around the defects you found, which you cannot guess at in advance.
F-3

Thirteen systems and seven model routes at pilot volume

The stack is ChatGPT, Codex, Claude, Gemini, Grok, Hermes, Composio, Buzz, HubSpot, ClickUp, Drive, GitHub and Apollo. Most carry per-seat or per-workspace cost. The routing table then assigns primary and fallback models across seven work types — but the pilot's stated volume is twenty scored accounts and five outreach records, which is far too little signal to distinguish model quality on any of those routes. You would be paying for optionality you cannot measure.

FixOne primary model family plus one fallback for the whole pilot; drop Gemini and Grok from the table until a specific measured shortfall justifies adding them. Hermes and Composio solve unattended scheduling and scoped OAuth for writes — real problems, but problems you do not have until an agent writes to a live system without a human present. Defer both to Wave 2 and run human-triggered sessions until then. Keep GitHub from day one; versioned configuration is cheap and the thing you will most regret skipping.
F-4

Demo and fulfillment are absent — and they conflict with the data rule

The stated phase-one scope is marketing & demo to fulfillment. The manual covers research, outreach, reply handling, meeting prep and onboarding status, then stops. There is no procedure for demo delivery, demo-to-proposal conversion, proposal production, compliance review of a delivered proposal, format and page-limit QA, or fulfillment capacity planning. That is the majority of the billable work and the largest single pool of delegable hours.

Writing it exposes the contradiction. The manual forbids agents from touching contract-sensitive material; white-glove proposal production for GovCon clients means working inside exactly that material — draft technical approaches, past performance narratives, pricing structure, and in many cases federal contract information. A blanket prohibition would exclude agents from the work you are selling.

FixReplace the binary with three data classes and bind each to an environment. CLASS-P — published solicitation text, agency budget documents, award history, client-approved boilerplate and capability statements: fully agent-accessible. CLASS-B — client business information that is neither FCI nor CUI, released to BCS under NDA: agent-accessible only in the named commercial workspace, with per-engagement client consent recorded in the work record. CLASS-R — FCI, CUI, pricing, reps and certifications, and anything a client has marked restricted: human-only, handled in the controlled environment, never entering a prompt. Then state the fulfillment tasks against those classes rather than against agent roles, and get client consent for CLASS-B in the engagement letter, not by exception later.
F-5

The approval funnel has no exit

Every first-touch message and every substantive reply requires human approval. At five sends a week that is fine. At fifty it consumes an owner. The manual anticipates this with campaign-level approval possible in the later-state column, but sets no criterion for getting there, so in practice the pilot posture becomes permanent and the team concludes agents did not save time.

FixMake graduation numeric and per-template rather than global. A message template moves from per-send approval to template-level approval after twenty consecutive approvals within its segment with no substantive rewrite; one factual defect, wrong-contact send, or duplicate resets the counter to zero and returns that template to per-send review. Same shape for content publication and internal CRM writes. Record the counter in the work ledger so it is a fact, not a feeling.
F-6

No economic model, so no way to know if it worked

The measurement table lists cost per qualified meeting — compare with prior acquisition methods, but no prior number is captured anywhere, and step B's baseline asks for delays, handoffs, and manual effort without requiring a unit. Nothing in the manual states a monthly spend cap, a target hours-reclaimed figure, or a break-even. The pilot can therefore neither pass nor fail.

FixMake step B produce three numbers per workflow stage: minutes per unit, units per week, and who does it today. Set the pilot's success threshold before starting — a specific figure for net weekly hours reclaimed and a hard monthly ceiling on tools and model usage combined. Section 04 below gives a first-pass estimate to work against; replace every row of it with observed times as step B completes.
F-7

Evaluation sets and emergency stop are required but undefined

Quality controls call for a small evaluation set with results meeting the approved threshold — no size, no threshold, no owner, no location. Step P tests an emergency stop that is never defined anywhere: not who can trigger it, not what it does, not how long recovery takes. Both are the kind of requirement that quietly does not happen on a three-person team, and both are what you reach for on your worst day.

FixFix the eval set at ten cases per workflow, stored beside the charter in GitHub, covering the nine conditions the manual already lists, with a stated pass threshold and a single command to run them. Define emergency stop concretely: any of the three hats can invoke it, it revokes the connected-account tokens and disables all schedules, it is one documented command, and restoring service requires the sponsor plus a written cause. Test it in step P and again quarterly with the restore test.
F-8

Deadline-bound work has no manual fallback, and outreach has no consent policy

Marketing has soft deadlines; fulfillment has hard ones. A proposal due date does not move because a runtime is down or a model is unavailable. The failure table handles model and tool failures generically but never states the rule that matters: no client deadline may depend on agent availability. Separately, the manual references consent status and a suppression flag without any underlying policy — nothing on CAN-SPAM requirements, sending-domain reputation, or the fact that automated LinkedIn actions violate the platform's terms and routinely cost accounts, which for a relationship-led GovCon practice is a real asset to lose.

FixAdd one sentence to the failure table: every fulfillment task an agent performs has a documented manual path, and the human owner confirms the manual path is viable before the deadline is committed. Add a short outreach compliance policy covering identification and opt-out in every message, a single authoritative suppression list checked pre-send, a separate warmed sending domain, and no automated LinkedIn actions — agents draft, a human sends from their own session.

The delegation map

This is the analysis the manual is missing. Every task in the three-phase plan was sorted on four questions: how often it recurs, whether a mistake is reversible, whether it needs judgment a rubric cannot hold, and what data class it touches. Tasks that recur often, fail reversibly, follow a rubric, and stay in CLASS-P are delegable now. Tasks that fail any one of those tests are not.

Gross across all three phases at steady state is roughly 43 hours a week. Charter maintenance, evaluation runs, change records, approval queue time and reading the watchdog digest cost eight to ten hours against that — the line the manual never books, and the reason its own "human review time" measure matters. Net is approximately 33 to 35 hours a week, a little under one full-time equivalent, spread across three domains.

Phase one alone is the number that should drive the decision to proceed: gross 28.5 hours without fulfillment, 35 with the compliance matrix included, against four to six hours of single-domain overhead. Net 23 to 29 hours a week — most of a person, recovered from a three-person team, without hiring. That is a real result, and it is concentrated in exactly two places: research-to-brief, and compliance matrix construction. If those two work, the programme works. If they do not, nothing downstream saves it.

Two honest caveats. These figures assume the volumes in the table, which a three-person team may not actually reach in early weeks — reclaimed hours scale with throughput, so low volume yields low return regardless of agent quality. And they assume review time stays at the "after" figures; a workflow that needs substantial rewriting rather than reviewing has negative return, which is precisely what the F-5 approval counter is built to detect.

05

Gaps by phase

Read against the three-phase plan rather than the manual's own structure. Phase one has the largest omission; phase three has the smallest agent surface and the highest liability.

PHASE 1

ProposalApp — marketing, demo, fulfillment

Marketing is well covered. Demo and fulfillment are not covered at all, and they are two-thirds of the stated scope. This is the section to write before anything is built, because fulfillment procedure determines the data model, and the data model determines what agents can be given.

Demo stage — absent

  • Qualification-to-demo criteria and booking flow
  • Tailored demo script from the approved claims register
  • Reminder and no-show recovery sequence
  • Recap, next-step draft, and demo-to-proposal conversion tracking
  • Demo outcome as a scored field, not a calendar event

Fulfillment stage — absent

  • Engagement kickoff and scope-of-work confirmation
  • Compliance matrix and proposal production workflow
  • Format, page-limit and completeness QA before submission
  • Version control and client review cycles
  • Capacity planning — how many concurrent engagements two or three people can carry
  • Deadline register with the manual-fallback rule from F-8
Resolve firstDecide whether ProposalApp white-glove means BCS staff produce proposal content on the client's behalf. If yes, CLASS-B consent belongs in the engagement letter, and the fulfillment procedure has to name which artifacts an agent may see. If no — if BCS only configures the product and coaches — the data question mostly disappears and fulfillment delegation is far simpler. The manual cannot be finished without this answer.
PHASE 2

BCS-CG — GovCon capture and teaming

The replication table is a reasonable sketch but thin on what GovCon capture actually requires. The good news is that the highest-value phase-two capability — compliance matrix construction — gets built during phase one for fulfillment. Build it as a domain-independent skill from the start and phase two arrives largely pre-built.

Add to the replication package

  • Solicitation watch across SAM.gov and agency forecasts, with amendment and Q&A deadline tracking
  • Incumbent and award-history research from public spending data
  • Vehicle and set-aside eligibility screening
  • Bid/no-bid packet assembly — agent assembles evidence, human decides
  • Past-performance library with written client releases
  • Teaming partner shortlists with capability and eligibility evidence

Sequencing correction

  • Step Y separates shared from domain-specific components near the end of the build. That is too late — by then the knowledge is entangled.
  • Externalize domain knowledge from the first charter: rubrics, claims, templates and source lists as data files, not as prose inside agent instructions.
  • Then replication is a configuration swap rather than a rewrite, and phase two costs days instead of weeks.
PHASE 3

CMMC CISO — GovCon implementation

The manual is right to activate this last, and right that the agent surface is narrow. Worth being explicit about why: nearly all CMMC delivery work is Tier 3 by construction. Scoping, SSP and POA&M authorship, evidence handling and assessment conclusions are CUI-bearing and professionally attributable. What remains for agents is the demand-generation and administrative layer — which is genuinely valuable and entirely safe, but it is not the delivery work.

Safe and worth building

  • Education content, FAQ and misconception library from public standards
  • Policy and procedure templates against public 800-171 control text, on synthetic examples
  • Awareness training and role-based enablement material
  • Readiness questionnaires and evidence request lists
  • Onboarding and intake status tracking, contents never seen

Two structural questions the manual should answer

  • BCS as an external service provider. If BCS handles client CUI in any capacity, BCS is itself in assessment scope, and every tool in the CUI path inherits that scope. This is the strongest argument for the manual's commercial/controlled split — state it as an architectural boundary with named environments, not as a handling rule.
  • Independence. If BCS holds or intends to hold an assessor role, providing consulting to an organization it later assesses raises a conflict the manual should address directly. Which role BCS occupies changes what phase three may sell, not just how agents support it.
Non-negotiableA commercial model processing CUI is a security incident with reporting obligations, not a policy violation to be corrected internally. Write it in those words. It is the one line in the whole manual whose violation could end the practice, and it should read like it.
06

Revised sequence

Same content as A–Z, reordered so evidence arrives before investment. Gates are conditions, not dates — a wave does not start because time passed.

Days 1–3

Thin slice

One task type: account research producing a sourced brief. Spreadsheet as the work record, an existing channel for approvals, no runtime, no OAuth gateway, no charter. Five real target accounts through it. Record actual minutes per brief and every defect. This is also step B's baseline, gathered for free.

Gate
Three of five briefs usable with light edits, and observed time per brief beats the manual baseline. If not, the problem is knowledge and rubric quality — fix that before building anything around it.
Weeks 1–4

Wave 1 — ProposalApp marketing, human-triggered

A–Z steps A, C, D, E, K, N, F and L, in that order: owners, data classification with the three CLASS tiers from F-4, offer, systems of record, knowledge, work schema, charters, skills. Research, outreach drafting, reply triage and demo briefs go live under per-send approval. GitHub from day one. No unattended writes, so no Hermes and no Composio yet. Ten-case eval set per workflow before each goes live.

Gate
Twenty scored accounts, five approved outreach records, zero sensitive-information events, and net hours reclaimed measured and positive.
Weeks 5–10

Wave 2 — Demo, fulfillment, and the runtime

Write the demo and fulfillment procedures that F-4 identified as missing. Build the compliance matrix skill as domain-independent from the outset. Now add Hermes and Composio, because scheduled unattended writes are finally the actual requirement. Watchdog as a single capped daily digest. First template graduations under the F-5 counter. Emergency stop defined and tested.

Gate
Two engagements fulfilled with agent-assisted production and no compliance defects. Defect rate declining for three consecutive weeks. Emergency stop tested end to end.
Weeks 11–16

Wave 3 — BCS-CG activation

Swap domain knowledge rather than rebuilding. Add solicitation watch, award-history research and teaming shortlists. Compliance matrix carries over unchanged if Wave 2 built it domain-independent. Second named owner joins, or Wave 3 waits.

Overlap rule
A new domain activates only when the prior domain has met its defect-rate and human-review-time targets for three consecutive weeks, or a named additional owner takes it. Sales pressure will push against this; the rule exists because two or three people cannot supervise three domains through their first defects at once.
Week 17+

Wave 4 — CMMC Growth, marketing layer only

Education content, prospecting, qualification, scheduling and intake status. Delivery work stays in the controlled environment with no agent involvement at any depth. Resolve the external-service-provider and independence questions from section 05 before selling, not after.

07

Before you finalize

Five answers change the shape of the manual. The first two change it materially.

  1. Are the two-to-three co-workers per project the same people across all three, or distinct?If the same, the overlap rule in section 06 is the most important line in the revision and the phase timeline stretches considerably. If distinct, the governance model needs per-domain owners and the role collapse in F-1 changes shape.
  2. Does ProposalApp white-glove mean BCS staff produce proposal content for clients?This determines whether CLASS-B exists, whether consent belongs in the engagement letter, and whether fulfillment is delegable at all. Everything in section 05 phase one depends on it.
  3. Does ProposalApp the product store client proposal data?If so there is a second, separate question — product security posture and what BCS commits to in its own terms — which is outside this manual's scope but should not be discovered later.
  4. Which CMMC role does BCS hold or intend to hold?Consultancy, registered provider, or assessor. It changes the independence constraints on phase three and therefore what the CISO practice can sell to whom.
  5. What is the actual monthly ceiling for tools and model usage combined?F-3 recommends deferring two systems and three models. That recommendation is only correct against a real number, and the manual currently has none.
