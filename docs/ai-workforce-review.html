Implementation review · Manual v1.0 · Revision 2

BCS AI Workforce Review

An assessment of the draft operating manual against the actual plan — ProposalApp marketing through fulfillment first, then BCS-CG GovCon, then CMMC CISO delivery — with a task-by-task judgment on what a two-to-three-person team should hand to agents and what it must keep.

Reviewed 8 Sep 2026 Source BCS_AI_Workforce_Implementation_Manual_1.docx Staffing assumed 2–3 humans + agents per project
Verdict

The manual is architecturally sound and operationally too heavy for the team that has to run it. Its core design decisions are the right ones and unusually well chosen: a hard CUI/FCI boundary declared before anything else, a shared work record with structured handoffs instead of passing chat history between models, and an approval matrix that separates drafting from sending from committing. Those three choices are what most AI-workforce plans get wrong, and they are why this plan is worth building on rather than rewriting.

Your answers resolve both blocking questions and, on balance, improve the outlook. Three separate teams of two to three means supervision capacity is roughly triple what I assumed, and the phases can overlap far more than the manual allows. The bottleneck is no longer headcount but the single shared platform role. Two things got harder rather than easier: some of those people are external to BCS, which the access model does not contemplate at all, and the CMMC market moved under the plan while it was being written.

The one finding that survives unchanged and unsoftened is the largest: the demo and fulfillment stages of ProposalApp are absent from the manual, and confirming that BCS staff author 80–90% of client proposal drafts makes that gap the centre of the whole programme rather than an omission at its edge. Fulfillment is where the delegation return is, where the data questions live, and where hard deadlines make agent reliability a client-facing risk for the first time.

01

What holds up under pressure

Five decisions in the draft should not be touched during revision. They are load-bearing.

  • The prohibition list is specific enough to be enforceable. Naming CUI, FCI, export-controlled data, credentials, network diagrams, vulnerability findings, SSPs and POA&Ms individually — rather than saying "sensitive information" — is what makes the rule testable. An agent can be evaluated against it.
  • Business state lives in systems of record, not in model memory. "Models do not exchange private chat histories" is the single most important sentence in the manual. It is what lets you swap a model, resume after a failure, and audit a decision six months later.
  • Autonomy is separated into four distinct authorities — draft, update internal records, send externally, approve commitments. Most plans conflate the last two and discover the problem when an agent quotes a price.
  • Every A–Z step carries completion evidence. Not "configure Composio" but "connection inventory and successful access tests." That converts a plan into something a sponsor can actually sign off.
  • "Human review time" is a tracked measure with the rule "automation must reduce total effort." This is the metric that catches the failure mode where agents produce more work than they remove. Keep it, and make it a gate rather than a report.
02

What breaks at three people

Eight findings, each with the smallest change that resolves it. Identifiers are for reference, not sequence — F-4 is the most consequential.

F-1

Nine roles, and now external people inside them

The manual assigns work to nine named owners. Three separate teams make that less crushing than it looked — but the roles still collapse per program, and one person remains the requester, the implementer, and the security approver of their own change. With three programs there is now a free fix the manual does not use: cross-program review. Program A's lead approves Program B's access and autonomy changes. Separation of duties without new headcount.

The larger issue is the one your answer introduced. Some of these six to nine people are external to BCS, and the access model does not contemplate non-employees anywhere. External personnel with access to HubSpot, the work ledger, agent configuration, and client proposal material is a third-party risk surface with no policy attached. For the CMMC program it is also a credentialing constraint: advisory work delivered under an RPO banner is performed by Registered Practitioners, so an external contractor doing that work needs to actually hold the credential, not merely be competent.

FixFour roles, not nine. SPONSOR (one, firm-wide: pricing, autonomy, production authorization), PLATFORM (one, shared across all three programs: agent config, evals, access, model routing, change control — do not triplicate this or configurations drift apart), and PROGRAM LEAD (three, one each: pipeline, sends, delivery, relationships). Security review is a duty assigned to a program lead from a different program, never to PLATFORM. Four actions still need a second named human: expanding write scope, expanding autonomy, first-touch sends, and resuming after a sensitive-information event.

Then add the external-personnel policy the manual is missing: executed NDA before any access; individually named identities with no shared logins; access scoped to one program namespace; offboarding with token revocation inside 24 hours and a named owner for it; client consent to subcontractors confirmed in the engagement letter before an external touches client material; and for CMMC advisory, RP credentials verified and recorded.
F-2

Twenty-six gates before the first unit of value

The A–Z sequence and the ten-day plan put the first approved outreach at day ten, and the first agent-produced deliverable of any kind at day seven. Everything before that is charter writing, namespace creation, OAuth configuration, and runtime deployment. For a small team, that is a large unpaid bet placed before anyone has seen whether an agent-written account brief is actually good enough to use.

FixInsert a seventy-two-hour thin slice ahead of step A: one specialist, one task type (account research producing a sourced brief), the work record as a spreadsheet, approvals as a message in an existing channel, no runtime, no Composio, no charter. Run five real target accounts through it. Then build the control plane around what actually worked — and around the defects you found, which you cannot guess at in advance.
F-3

Nothing you currently pay for can run the automation

This is the correction that matters most in the budget, and it is easy to miss. ChatGPT Pro, Claude Max, and Claude Team seats are human-interface subscriptions. They are not programmatic access, and they do not authorize a scheduled process to run unattended against your systems at three in the morning. Hermes and Composio both need API credentials billed by usage — so the agent workforce is a new line item, not something the existing $630 a month already covers. Any plan that assumes otherwise under-budgets the whole programme.

The good news is that the new line item is small at your volumes — the estimate in section 06 lands around $150–300 a month for phase one, dominated almost entirely by research. The routing table is still over-built: seven work types with primary and fallback models, against a pilot of twenty scored accounts and five outreach records. That is far too little signal to tell those routes apart, and you would be paying in complexity rather than dollars.

FixBudget agent API usage explicitly, separately from seats. Collapse the routing table to one primary model plus one fallback — which still satisfies the manual's own rule that an independent reviewer must not rely solely on the drafting model, and that rule is the only real argument for keeping a second vendor. Retire the routes you cannot measure. Keep GitHub from day one; versioned configuration is nearly free and the thing you would most regret skipping. Hermes and Composio are worth adding, but at Wave 2, when unattended scheduled writes are actually the requirement rather than an anticipated one.
F-4

Fulfillment is the service, and the manual stops before it

You confirmed the model: BCS authors 80–90% ready drafts, augments the client's proposal team, and syncs weekly. The manual covers research, outreach, reply handling, meeting prep and onboarding status — then stops. There is no procedure for demo delivery, demo-to-proposal conversion, proposal production, compliance review, format and page-limit QA, version control through client review cycles, the weekly sync package, or fulfillment capacity planning. That is the billable work and the largest single pool of delegable hours.

On the data question, the answer is less alarming than the manual's framing implies. Federal contract information is defined as information provided by or generated for the Government under a contract — which generally means post-award. Pre-award proposal drafting is therefore mostly not an FCI obligation at all; it is a confidentiality and competition-sensitivity obligation owed to your client under NDA. That is a contractual duty, not a regulatory one, and it is far more tractable.

The exception is narrow and worth targeting precisely: past-performance narratives describing work under existing contracts, and any material a client shares as an incumbent preparing a recompete. That subset can carry FCI, and occasionally CUI. Treat it as the controlled slice rather than treating all proposal work as suspect.

FixThree data classes bound to environments, replacing the binary. CLASS-P — published solicitation text, agency budget documents, award history, client-approved boilerplate and capability statements: fully agent-accessible. CLASS-B — client business information released to BCS under NDA and neither FCI nor CUI: agent-accessible in the named commercial workspace, with per-engagement client consent recorded in the work record. CLASS-R — past-performance and incumbent-derived material pending determination, plus all FCI, CUI, pricing and certifications: human-only, in the controlled environment, never in a prompt.

Then make the determination a step rather than an assumption: at engagement kickoff, ask the client in writing which material they consider FCI or restricted, and default to CLASS-R for anything unanswered. Work in the client's environment wherever they will allow it — their controls apply, and your scope stays small. Write the fulfillment procedure against these classes rather than against agent roles.
F-5

The approval funnel has no exit

Every first-touch message and every substantive reply requires human approval. At five sends a week that is fine. At fifty it consumes an owner. The manual anticipates this with campaign-level approval possible in the later-state column, but sets no criterion for getting there, so in practice the pilot posture becomes permanent and the team concludes agents did not save time.

FixMake graduation numeric and per-template rather than global. A message template moves from per-send approval to template-level approval after twenty consecutive approvals within its segment with no substantive rewrite; one factual defect, wrong-contact send, or duplicate resets the counter to zero and returns that template to per-send review. Same shape for content publication and internal CRM writes. Record the counter in the work ledger so it is a fact, not a feeling.
F-6

No economic model, so no way to know if it worked

The measurement table lists cost per qualified meeting — compare with prior acquisition methods, but no prior number is captured anywhere, and step B's baseline asks for delays, handoffs, and manual effort without requiring a unit. Nothing in the manual states a monthly spend cap, a target hours-reclaimed figure, or a break-even. The pilot can therefore neither pass nor fail.

FixMake step B produce three numbers per workflow stage: minutes per unit, units per week, and who does it today. Set the pilot's success threshold before starting — a specific figure for net weekly hours reclaimed and a hard monthly ceiling on tools and model usage combined. Section 04 below gives a first-pass estimate to work against; replace every row of it with observed times as step B completes.
F-7

Evaluation sets and emergency stop are required but undefined

Quality controls call for a small evaluation set with results meeting the approved threshold — no size, no threshold, no owner, no location. Step P tests an emergency stop that is never defined anywhere: not who can trigger it, not what it does, not how long recovery takes. Both are the kind of requirement that quietly does not happen on a three-person team, and both are what you reach for on your worst day.

FixFix the eval set at ten cases per workflow, stored beside the charter in GitHub, covering the nine conditions the manual already lists, with a stated pass threshold and a single command to run them. Define emergency stop concretely: any of the three hats can invoke it, it revokes the connected-account tokens and disables all schedules, it is one documented command, and restoring service requires the sponsor plus a written cause. Test it in step P and again quarterly with the restore test.
F-8

Deadline-bound work has no fallback, and the LinkedIn risk is already running

Marketing has soft deadlines; proposal fulfillment has hard ones. A submission date does not move because a runtime is down. The failure table handles model and tool failures generically but never states the rule that matters: no client deadline may depend on agent availability.

The outreach risk is no longer hypothetical. SalesRobot plus two LinkedIn accounts is LinkedIn automation, running now. It violates LinkedIn's terms, and the standard consequence is account restriction or permanent ban. For a relationship-led GovCon practice those accounts and connection graphs are a real asset, and for a firm whose product is compliance credibility, running tooling that breaks a platform's terms is an awkward position to be in if a client ever asks. That is a judgment call and yours to make — but it should be a decision on the record, not a default.

FixAdd one line to the failure table: every fulfillment task an agent performs has a documented manual path, and the program lead confirms that path is viable before the deadline is committed to a client.

On outreach: decide explicitly whether the LinkedIn accounts are disposable. If they are not, move to agent-drafted and human-sent — the drafting is where nearly all the time goes, so you keep most of the leverage and lose the ban risk. Either way, add the outreach compliance policy the manual lacks: identification and opt-out in every message, one authoritative suppression list checked pre-send, and a separate warmed sending domain so cold outreach cannot damage the deliverability of client correspondence.
F-9

Personal accounts are holding business data, and the product makes a promise

Two items in the current stack are personal accounts — Gemini and Perplexity, described as personal. Business and client-adjacent material flowing through consumer-tier personal accounts means no administrative control, no audit visibility, consumer retention terms rather than negotiated ones, and nothing to revoke when someone leaves. With external personnel now in scope, that last point is the sharp one. This is precisely the finding BCS would write up in a client's readiness assessment.

Separately, "no one will have access except them" is a strong representation to make about ProposalApp. GovCon buyers will test it, and a compliance-adjacent vendor gets held to a higher standard than a generic SaaS. It needs to be technically true — tenant isolation, no routine operator read access, and a documented break-glass procedure if one exists — and it needs to be in the terms rather than in a sales conversation. It also needs an ingest-side control, because a client will eventually upload something they should not, whatever the policy says.

FixMove every tool holding business data onto org-managed plans with named accounts and an offboarding path, or stop using it for business work. For ProposalApp, write the access representation into the terms of service, confirm it matches the implementation, and add the manual's own sensitive-information procedure at the product layer: detect a likely CUI or FCI upload, stop, notify, and route the client to their own controlled channel — without BCS staff reading the content to make that determination.
F-10

The CMMC ground moved in July, and agents will not know

On 13 July 2026 the Department suspended CMMC Phase II requirements, which would have taken effect on 10 November 2026 and required Level 2 third-party assessments for contractors handling CUI. A reform task force was convened to review the program. Phase I remains fully in effect — self-assessments, DFARS 252.204-7012, NIST SP 800-171 compliance, SPRS score posting and annual affirmations all continue unchanged.

This is a live hazard for exactly the work you plan to delegate. An agent drafting CMMC education content today, working from training data or any source written before July, will state the November deadline and the third-party assessment requirement with complete confidence. Published by a firm selling CMMC advisory services, that error is worse than unhelpful — it is the single most credibility-damaging mistake available in this market, and the manual's "every fact needs a source" rule does not catch it, because a stale source is still a source.

FixAdd a volatile-claims register to the knowledge base: the specific claims that must be re-verified against a primary source before any publication, each with its primary-source URL and a last-verified date. Program status, deadlines, phase requirements and assessment obligations all belong on it. Then tighten the sourcing rule for this class of claim: a regulatory assertion requires a source dated after the most recent program change, not merely a source. Tie the knowledge review cadence to program events rather than to a fixed quarterly date — the task force outcome will land inside your build window.

Gross across all three phases at steady state is roughly 43 hours a week. Charter maintenance, evaluation runs, change records, approval queue time and reading the watchdog digest cost eight to ten hours against that — the line the manual never books, and the reason its own "human review time" measure matters. Net is approximately 33 to 35 hours a week, a little under one full-time equivalent, spread across three domains.

Phase one alone is the number that should drive the decision to proceed: gross 28.5 hours without fulfillment, 35 with the compliance matrix included, against four to six hours of single-domain overhead. Net 23 to 29 hours a week — most of a person, recovered from a three-person team, without hiring. That is a real result, and it is concentrated in exactly two places: research-to-brief, and compliance matrix construction. If those two work, the programme works. If they do not, nothing downstream saves it.

Two honest caveats. These figures assume the volumes in the table, which a three-person team may not actually reach in early weeks — reclaimed hours scale with throughput, so low volume yields low return regardless of agent quality. And they assume review time stays at the "after" figures; a workflow that needs substantial rewriting rather than reviewing has negative return, which is precisely what the F-5 approval counter is built to detect.

05

Gaps by phase

Read against the three-phase plan rather than the manual's own structure. Phase one has the largest omission; phase three has the smallest agent surface and the highest liability.

PHASE 1

ProposalApp — marketing, demo, fulfillment

Marketing is well covered. Demo and fulfillment are not covered at all, and they are two-thirds of the stated scope. This is the section to write before anything is built, because fulfillment procedure determines the data model, and the data model determines what agents can be given.

Demo stage — absent

  • Qualification-to-demo criteria and booking flow
  • Tailored demo script from the approved claims register
  • Reminder and no-show recovery sequence
  • Recap, next-step draft, and demo-to-proposal conversion tracking
  • Demo outcome as a scored field, not a calendar event

Fulfillment stage — absent

  • Engagement kickoff and scope-of-work confirmation
  • Compliance matrix and proposal production workflow
  • Format, page-limit and completeness QA before submission
  • Version control and client review cycles
  • Capacity planning — how many concurrent engagements two or three people can carry
  • Deadline register with the manual-fallback rule from F-8
Resolve firstDecide whether ProposalApp white-glove means BCS staff produce proposal content on the client's behalf. If yes, CLASS-B consent belongs in the engagement letter, and the fulfillment procedure has to name which artifacts an agent may see. If no — if BCS only configures the product and coaches — the data question mostly disappears and fulfillment delegation is far simpler. The manual cannot be finished without this answer.
PHASE 2

BCS-CG — GovCon capture and teaming

The replication table is a reasonable sketch but thin on what GovCon capture actually requires. The good news is that the highest-value phase-two capability — compliance matrix construction — gets built during phase one for fulfillment. Build it as a domain-independent skill from the start and phase two arrives largely pre-built.

Add to the replication package

  • Solicitation watch across SAM.gov and agency forecasts, with amendment and Q&A deadline tracking
  • Incumbent and award-history research from public spending data
  • Vehicle and set-aside eligibility screening
  • Bid/no-bid packet assembly — agent assembles evidence, human decides
  • Past-performance library with written client releases
  • Teaming partner shortlists with capability and eligibility evidence

Sequencing correction

  • Step Y separates shared from domain-specific components near the end of the build. That is too late — by then the knowledge is entangled.
  • Externalize domain knowledge from the first charter: rubrics, claims, templates and source lists as data files, not as prose inside agent instructions.
  • Then replication is a configuration swap rather than a rewrite, and phase two costs days instead of weeks.
  • With a separate BCS-CG team, the domain knowledge work — rubrics, ICP, claims register, source lists, past-performance releases — can start in week one. It needs no platform capacity, so it does not compete with ProposalApp for the one shared role.
PHASE 3

CMMC CISO — advisory, RP/RPO, separate tooling

Your answers make this the best-designed of the three programs, and the one whose market changed most. Separate tooling from ProposalApp is exactly right. The RP/RPO positioning carries no independence conflict — RPOs advise and prepare, they do not assess, so the C3PAO problem simply does not arise. And because a cleanly scoped advisory engagement that never receives CUI generally does not make BCS an external service provider, the Tier 3 boundary is what keeps BCS off the assessment path entirely.

The agent surface stays narrow, and it is worth being explicit about why: nearly all CMMC delivery is human-only by construction. Scoping, SSP and POA&M authorship, evidence handling and assessment conclusions are CUI-bearing and professionally attributable. What agents get is the demand-generation and administrative layer — genuinely valuable, entirely safe, and not the delivery work.

Safe and worth building

  • Education content, FAQ and misconception library from public standards
  • Policy and procedure templates against public 800-171 control text, on synthetic examples
  • Awareness training and role-based enablement material
  • Readiness questionnaires and evidence request lists
  • Onboarding and intake status tracking, contents never seen
  • SPRS score and annual-affirmation deadline tracking — dates and status only

The market moved — reconsider the sequencing

  • Phase II suspension removed the forcing function BCS was likely counting on: no November deadline, no imminent Level 2 third-party assessment requirement.
  • But Phase I is untouched. Self-assessments, SPRS scores and annual affirmations by a senior official all continue — and an affirmation is a personal attestation with False Claims Act exposure behind it.
  • That is advisory demand, not assessment-prep demand. It maps precisely onto fractional CISO and RP services, which is what you chose. The suspension arguably improved your positioning relative to a certification-prep practice.
  • So the case for activating CMMC last is weaker than it was. The confusion itself is the content opportunity, and it has a shelf life — the reform task force will report inside your build window.
Non-negotiableA commercial model processing CUI is a security incident with reporting obligations, not an internal policy slip. Write it in those words. It is the one line whose violation could end the practice — and, given the ESP scoping above, also the line that keeps BCS out of assessment scope. It should read like both.

Revised sequence

Reordered so evidence arrives before investment — and now parallelized, because three separate teams changed the constraint. The bottleneck is no longer team capacity. It is the one shared PLATFORM role, and every gate below is written against that.

Days 1–3

Thin slice — ProposalApp only

One task type: account research producing a sourced brief. Spreadsheet work record, approvals in an existing channel, no runtime, no OAuth gateway, no charter. Five real target accounts. Record actual minutes per brief and every defect — this is also the manual's step B baseline, gathered for free, and it replaces the estimates in section 04 with facts.

Gate
Three of five briefs usable with light edits, and observed time per brief beats the manual baseline. If not, the problem is rubric and knowledge quality — fix that before building anything around it.
Weeks 1–4

Wave 1 — ProposalApp marketing, plus two parallel tracks

ProposalApp team: A–Z steps A, C, D, E, K, N, F and L in that order — owners, the three data classes from F-4, offer, systems of record, knowledge, work schema, charters, skills. Research, outreach drafting, reply triage and demo briefs go live under per-send approval. Ten-case eval set per workflow before each goes live. GitHub from day one; no unattended writes yet, so no Hermes or Composio.

In parallel, needing no platform capacity: the BCS-CG team builds domain knowledge — rubrics, ICP, claims register, source lists, past-performance releases. The CMMC team builds the volatile-claims register from F-10 and drafts education content against verified primary sources. Neither competes with ProposalApp for PLATFORM.

Gate
Twenty scored accounts, five approved outreach records, zero sensitive-information events, and net reclaimed hours measured and positive. External-personnel access policy written and applied before any external touches client material.
Weeks 5–10

Wave 2 — Demo, fulfillment, and the runtime

Write the demo and fulfillment procedures that F-4 identified as missing — the highest-value work in the whole plan. Build the compliance-matrix skill domain-independent from the first line, because BCS-CG inherits it unchanged. Now add Hermes and Composio, since scheduled unattended writes are finally the actual requirement. Watchdog as a single capped daily digest. First template graduations under the F-5 counter. Emergency stop defined and tested.

Gate
Two engagements fulfilled with agent-assisted production and no compliance defects. Defect rate declining three consecutive weeks. Emergency stop tested end to end. Every fulfillment task has a documented manual fallback.
Weeks 8–14

Wave 3 — BCS-CG activation

Can start before Wave 2 closes, since the team is distinct — the constraint is PLATFORM having closed ProposalApp's defect backlog, not ProposalApp being finished. Swap domain knowledge rather than rebuilding. Add solicitation watch, award-history research and teaming shortlists. The compliance matrix carries over unchanged if Wave 2 built it properly.

Revised overlap rule
The original rule waited on the prior program's team. With distinct teams that is the wrong constraint: a new program activates when PLATFORM has closed the prior program's open defects, not when the prior team is free. Domain knowledge work never waits — only agent activation does.
Weeks 8–16

Wave 4 — CMMC Growth, and worth pulling forward

Education content, prospecting, qualification, scheduling and intake status. Delivery stays human-only at every depth. The case for going last is weaker than it was: Phase I obligations are untouched and the advisory demand behind them maps onto RP and fractional-CISO work precisely, while the market is confused about what the July suspension did and did not change. That confusion is a content opportunity with a shelf life. The content track can run from week one at negligible platform cost; consider activating outreach as soon as the volatile-claims register is in place.

08

What to decide next

The five blocking questions are answered. These are the decisions those answers created — smaller, but each one gates something.

  1. Are the LinkedIn accounts disposable?This is the only genuinely uncomfortable finding in the review and it is already running. If the accounts and their connection graphs matter to a relationship-led practice, move to agent-drafted and human-sent; you keep nearly all the time saving, since drafting is where the hours are. Either way, make it a recorded decision rather than an inherited default.
  2. Who is the single PLATFORM owner, and do they have capacity for three programs?Every gate in section 07 depends on this one role. Triplicating it causes configuration drift across programs; under-resourcing it makes it the bottleneck for all three. If no one can hold it across three programs, that is the real argument for sequencing rather than parallelizing.
  3. Should CMMC move ahead of BCS-CG?The suspension improved the fit between the current market and the advisory positioning you chose. The content track costs almost nothing to start and the window is open now. This is a go-to-market call, not an architecture one — but the manual's sequencing assumes an answer it no longer has evidence for.
  4. What is the standing FCI determination process per engagement?Pre-award proposal content is largely not FCI, but past-performance and incumbent recompete material can be. Ask the client in writing at kickoff, default unanswered material to CLASS-R, and record the determination in the work record. Cheap to do at kickoff, expensive to reconstruct later.
  5. Which model vendors survive consolidation?Five vendors today, two of them personal accounts. The manual's own independent-review rule justifies exactly two for agent work. Human seats are a separate question and preferences are legitimate — but the personal accounts should go regardless, on governance grounds rather than cost.
